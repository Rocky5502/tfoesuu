\begin{center}{\large\bfseries Abstract}\end{center}
Software updates must preserve fairness even when repeated executions are random. We study the number of program executions required to certify that a patch has not introduced a fairness regression, given a prior certificate, sound change-impact information, and explicitly specified random-tape replay access. A counterfactual graph couples fairness comparisons while old/new replay exposes patch effects rather than absolute outcomes.
For Gaussian paired differences, an uncertainty-weighted graph allocation $\Psi(H,\nu)$ bounds fixed-design testing and adaptive null information. For Bernoulli threshold programs, four-way counterfactual replay changes the small-effect dependence from quadratic to linear, but a fixed positive fairness tolerance restores quadratic scaling. Partial replay exposes distinct edge-witness and active-vertex fractional covers; matched-background controls recover linear scaling in a scoped heterogeneous family. We also distinguish a fixed-allocation familywise logarithm from sequential expected-null savings. The bounds hold under declared null classes, cost units, and replay semantics; unrestricted heterogeneous and composite-null optimality remains open.
Our validation suite uses deterministic theorem fixtures, exact finite-law oracle checks, and a seven-population analysis of a historical protected-group encoding defect. The parent revision exhibits nonzero discrepancies for two populations, whereas the repaired revision agrees with a canonical group encoding for all seven. These finite-row software-behavior comparisons neither estimate causal discrimination nor validate the replay-complexity bounds. Our contribution is a theorem-driven account of when software-evolution information and replay alter the cost of fairness regression certification.
